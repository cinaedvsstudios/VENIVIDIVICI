The men guarding Nerva's palace had not forgotten Domittianne.

For years, they had served an emperor who took a close interest in the army and had increased the rewards of military service. Whatever the senators said about him, the soldiers' experience of his reign could be very different.

A Praetorian returning to his barracks after a watch at the palace still belonged to the same military community he had known before the assassination. His equipment, duties and fellow soldiers remained familiar. But the man whose safety he was sworn to protect had changed, and the emperor he had once served was now officially condemned.

The conflict was more than a question of sentiment.

Domittianne had been killed by men connected with the imperial household and government. To the senators who had suffered under his rule, those men could appear to be the instruments of deliverance.

To soldiers who had served and respected him, they were responsible for the murder of their commander.

Immediately after his death, the troops had shown how deeply they felt the loss. Some had wanted Domittianne to be honoured as a god, and there were calls for the people involved in the assassination to be punished.

At first, those demands had lacked effective leadership.

The new government continued, and the Senate set about removing the dead emperor's name and images from public life.

But the grievance endured.

The Praetorian Guard occupied a special place in the Roman system. Unlike the legions guarding distant provinces, its soldiers were stationed in and around the capital. Their duty was to protect the emperor and provide an armed presence close to the centre of government.

The position offered prestige, privileges and opportunities not available to an ordinary legionary serving far from Rome.

It also placed powerful soldiers within reach of political disputes that were usually settled by senators and imperial officials.

The emperor's authority depended upon the army. Yet the armed men closest to his person could become a threat if they ceased to accept his commands.

Nerva was discovering the danger of that arrangement.

His own experience had been shaped by decades in public office. He knew the Senate, the great families of Rome, and the complicated relationships of the imperial court. He could consult men of influence, revise policy and make promises about the protection of their lives.

None of this gave him the affection that Domittianne had enjoyed among many soldiers.

And it did not make the Guard forget the manner in which its previous master had died.

Even Nerva's coinage reflected an awareness of the problem. Some pieces proclaimed harmony between the armies, presenting an image of the unity an emperor needed.

The words were reassuring.

But they were also a reminder that harmony could not be taken for granted.

In the year ninety-seven, the danger became much more immediate.

A man named Kasperius Ayleeanus emerged at the centre of the Guard's discontent.

He was no stranger to the imperial household. Ayleeanus had held authority among the Praetorians under Domittianne, and during Nerva's reign he again occupied the powerful position of Praetorian prefect.

A prefect commanded troops whose presence could determine whether the emperor's orders were obeyed in the capital itself.

In ordinary circumstances, such a commander was supposed to be one of the ruler's most dependable servants.

Ayleeanus used his position very differently.

He encouraged the soldiers to demand punishment for those responsible for Domittianne's death.

With an experienced commander giving direction to their anger, the Praetorians possessed something they had lacked in the days immediately following the assassination.

They had leadership.

The demands they brought before Nerva concerned men who were already familiar within the palace.

One was Parthenius.

In the final years of Domittianne's reign, Parthenius had held an important position in the imperial household. He was a chamberlain, a man whose duties brought him close to the emperor and gave him considerable influence over access to the private apartments.

Such a position required trust.

The daily routine of the palace depended upon officials who knew when visitors could be admitted, how messages were delivered, and which matters could be brought directly to the ruler's attention.

Parthenius had been one of those officials.

Yet he had also been involved in the conspiracy that brought Domittianne's reign to its violent end.

For the soldiers demanding vengeance, his position made the betrayal especially serious.

Here was a man who had lived within the emperor's own household, whose authority had depended upon imperial favour, and who had helped bring about the death of the person he served.

The other prominent target was Petronius Sekundus.

Petronius had been a Praetorian prefect at the time of Domittianne's assassination. He belonged to the very military command upon which the emperor should have been able to rely for his protection.

His involvement in the change of government gave the soldiers another reason for anger.

The conspiracy had not simply come from enemies outside the palace.

Men entrusted with the emperor's safety and service had helped overthrow him.

To the Senate, their actions had opened the way for Nerva.

To the Praetorians, the same actions demanded punishment.

This was the contradiction at the heart of the new reign.

Nerva occupied the throne because Domittianne was dead.

But the soldiers responsible for guarding that throne wanted revenge upon the people who had made Nerva's accession possible.

And now they had a commander prepared to press their demands.

By the autumn of ninety-seven, the grievance had become a mutiny.

Armed Praetorians confronted the emperor at the imperial residence and demanded that the surviving men involved in Domittianne's murder be surrendered to them.

This was no ordinary petition.

The men making the demand carried the weapons upon which the emperor's own security depended.

For Nerva, agreement would mean sacrificing people associated with the transfer of power that had brought him to the throne. Refusal meant resisting soldiers who had already demonstrated that they were willing to challenge his authority.

The man who had promised the Senate relief from arbitrary executions now faced an extraordinary reversal.

He had wanted to end the practice of men being destroyed because an emperor or his court demanded it.

Instead, armed soldiers were demanding that the emperor himself deliver men to their deaths.

Nerva resisted.

An ancient account describes him confronting the soldiers with remarkable determination. He is said to have exposed his throat, offering them his own life rather than yielding to their demands.

The scene has survived because it shows how far the confrontation had gone.

An emperor stood before the men who were supposed to protect him and, if the account is accepted, invited them to kill him instead.

It was an act of personal defiance.

But it was not enough.

The soldiers did not abandon their demands.

Nor did the authority of the imperial office compel them to obey.

In the end, the men Ayleeanus wanted were killed.

Petronius Sekundus was put to death, and Parthenius also fell into the hands of the soldiers. Later accounts describe particularly brutal treatment of the former chamberlain before his death.

For the Praetorians, vengeance had been carried out.

For the men who had helped remove Domittianne, the change of emperor had offered no lasting protection.

And for Nerva, the consequences reached far beyond the deaths themselves.

He had not merely lost an argument with his guards.

He had been forced to accept the destruction of men whose fate he had tried to control, in a confrontation carried out within the very centre of imperial power.

The emperor could appoint officials, issue commands and sit before the Senate as the first citizen of Rome.

But the armed men who should have obeyed him had imposed their will upon him.

An ordinary member of the imperial household, hearing that the guards had obtained the deaths they demanded, might have drawn a simple and disturbing conclusion.

The emperor's word was not always enough.

That uncertainty could spread without the need for another soldier to lift a weapon.

Officials whose work depended upon imperial instructions had reason to wonder whose orders would prevail in the next dispute. Senators who had welcomed Nerva as the answer to Domittianne's harshness could see that his moderation did not guarantee his security.

Even the soldiers themselves had been taught a dangerous lesson.

Their refusal to obey had succeeded.

The problem was not that Nerva had lost the formal powers of his office. No new emperor had been proclaimed, and the Senate had not withdrawn its recognition.

He remained the ruler of the Roman world.

But a political position could be weakened even when its titles remained intact.

The mutiny revealed that the forces supporting the government were not united, and that the emperor could not be certain of controlling the troops closest to his own residence.

For a man with Nerva's background, the humiliation was particularly severe.

He had spent much of his career surviving the struggles of more powerful rulers. He knew that emperors could fall, and that a change of government could bring both reward and danger.

He had been old enough to remember the civil wars following Nero's death, when successive claimants had been raised up and cast down by the armies.

The recollection of that period was not simply a story about the distant past.

It was a warning about what could happen when political authority and military obedience separated.

The Praetorians were only a small part of Rome's armed forces.

Far beyond the capital, the great legions still stood along the Rhine, the Danube and the eastern frontiers.

Their commanders possessed military experience, established reputations and the loyalty of troops under their immediate authority.

Most of those soldiers would never see Nerva in person.

They would know him through official letters, imperial portraits, coins and the orders passed down through their own commanders.

For an emperor without a celebrated military career, these connections mattered enormously.

His government had to be accepted by men whose lives were spent far from the political world in which he had earned his reputation.

And after the mutiny, the weakness of his position could no longer be hidden behind the appearance of agreement in Rome.

Nerva was already an elderly man.

His health was poor, and there was no son waiting to inherit the throne.

The absence of an immediate successor had been part of his position from the beginning, but the events at the palace now made it much more pressing.

An emperor who could not command the loyalty of the Guard had little reason to believe that a disputed succession would be settled peacefully.

And if Nerva died without securing a successor, the men commanding the legions might once again find themselves deciding the fate of Rome.

The memory of Verjinnius Rufus was especially relevant.

Thirty years earlier, soldiers had tried to raise that commander to the imperial throne. He had refused, insisting that the decision belonged to the Roman people and their Senate.

But not every general would make the same choice.

Nerva had experienced the failure of promises to restrain armed men who believed they had a right to decide who lived and died.

What he needed now was not another promise.

Nor was it enough to surround himself with senators who respected his moderation.

He needed to strengthen the government in a way that the army itself would recognise.

The solution could not come from restoring Domittianne's rule or punishing every soldier who had taken part in the mutiny. Either course threatened to revive the very conflict Nerva was trying to contain.

He needed to give the empire a future that powerful commanders and ordinary soldiers could accept.

And that meant confronting the question he had been able to leave unanswered when he first became emperor.

Who would rule after him?

Somewhere beyond Rome, among the frontier armies, there were men whose authority rested upon years of military service rather than the approval of the Senate.

One of them would soon become central to Nerva's future.

For the ageing emperor, the task was no longer simply to govern the empire he had inherited.

It was to choose a successor strong enough to keep it together.
