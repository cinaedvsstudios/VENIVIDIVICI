For some of the people travelling towards Rome, the new emperor's authority meant something much more immediate than an oath given in the Senate.

It meant that they could go home.

During Domittianne's reign, men had been sent away from the capital on charges that could be as dangerous to their families as to themselves. Some had been accused of threatening the emperor's authority. Others had fallen victim to political hostility, private rivalries, or the suspicions that flourished around the imperial court.

Exile did not always mean a life of poverty, and the experience was different for every family. But it meant separation from familiar places, from friends and relations, and sometimes from the property upon which an entire household depended.

A traveller returning after several years might recognise the shape of the hills, then the roads and buildings he had known before his departure. The people who had remained in Rome would have changed. A familiar house might belong to somebody else. And the person coming home could not be sure whether the welcome he expected would be waiting.

Under Nerva, those banished by the previous government were permitted to return.

The decision was one of the most visible signs that the old atmosphere of suspicion was being challenged.

Those facing prosecution for offences against the emperor's dignity were released from their trials, and new accusations of that kind were forbidden. A charge which had once threatened a man's property, reputation and life could no longer be used in quite the same way.

For the people who had lived under the fear of prosecution, the distinction was considerable.

Yet permission to return was only the beginning. Some people had lost estates, houses or possessions which the imperial government had taken into its own hands. Nerva ordered the restoration of property that could still be recovered from the imperial treasury.

It was a generous policy, but not a simple one.

Land could have passed to new owners. Money could have been spent. The records of a confiscation might be easier to find than the property itself.

In an administrative office, a clerk examining the claims of returning citizens would have faced a very practical difficulty. He could place a name beside an entry in a register, but that did not mean the objects named in it were still available to be returned.

The new emperor could promise a change in policy. He could not undo every consequence of the years before him.

And the men who had helped enforce those earlier policies now faced troubles of their own.

Under Domittianne, accusations had become a means by which one person could threaten another. Informers might gain advantage by reporting remarks or behaviour that could be interpreted as hostility towards the emperor.

A neighbour's words, a private disagreement, or the religious practices of a household might become the beginning of a dangerous investigation.

When Nerva changed the law, resentment against those who had made accusations did not simply disappear. It turned in the opposite direction.

The ancient accounts describe a wave of new charges, with people denouncing one another in a climate that had become difficult to control.

Some of the former informers were themselves condemned.

The reversal was not equally gentle to everybody. Nerva's government also imposed severe penalties upon slaves and freed people who had conspired against their masters, and the right to accuse a master was sharply restricted.

The freedom celebrated by senators was not freedom shared equally by everyone who lived in Rome.

Among the political class, however, the fear of the old trials was giving way to a different anxiety.

Fronto, a consul of the time, was remembered for an observation about the problem. He reportedly considered it bad to live under a ruler who allowed nobody any freedom, but worse to live under one who permitted absolutely everything.

The point was not a defence of Domittianne.

It was a warning that the sudden removal of restraint could create dangers of its own.

Nerva listened to such concerns and sought to bring the accusations under control.

He had promised that senators would no longer be executed at an emperor's pleasure. That promise mattered most if it could survive a moment when the emperor himself had reason to feel threatened.

But to protect people from arbitrary punishment was only one part of governing.

The households of Rome still needed food and shelter. Public business still required money. And every decision to return property or ease a burden had consequences for the resources available to the state.

Nerva began taking measures intended to benefit people who had little influence in the Senate.

Among them was a scheme to provide land for poorer Romans. Senators were appointed to oversee purchases and distribution, with a sum of sixty million sesterces set aside for the purpose.

It was an enormous commitment.

For a family with no property of its own, a small piece of land could mean a different kind of future. But to make such a policy work, officials had to find suitable estates, establish what they cost, arrange their purchase and determine how the land would be allotted.

The business of generosity quickly became the business of paperwork, money and difficult choices.

Nerva's government was making other changes as well. He reduced some of the costly ceremonies and spectacles that had consumed public funds, including certain sacrifices and horse races.

The measures did not mean that Rome ceased to enjoy public entertainment. They reflected an attempt to reduce expenditure while supporting policies the new emperor considered important.

The money still had to come from somewhere.

Eventually, valuable possessions associated with the imperial residence were offered for sale. Precious vessels, fine furnishings, clothing, houses and estates could all be turned into funds.

An object that had stood within the palace as a sign of imperial wealth might now find itself in the hands of a buyer who could afford it.

The ancient historian who records the sales also describes Nerva as unwilling to bargain fiercely over the price. The purpose, it seems, was not to extract the greatest possible amount from every purchase.

Nerva had also forbidden the erection of new statues of himself in gold or silver.

It was another deliberate contrast with the public honours that had surrounded Domittianne.

Yet financial moderation did not prevent the emperor from using coins to announce his achievements.

For most of Rome's inhabitants, a coin was a far more familiar object than an imperial proclamation. Money passed through markets, workshops and households. It travelled in the hands of people who would never attend a meeting of the Senate.

And some of Nerva's coins proclaimed changes that affected lives well beyond the capital.

In Italy, communities had long been required to assist the imperial system of transport and communication. Official travellers and messengers needed animals, vehicles, accommodation and provisions as they moved along the roads.

For people responsible for supplying those services, the demands could be expensive and disruptive.

A farmer who needed his animals for work could hardly regard their requisition for an official journey as a small inconvenience.

Nerva relieved Italian communities of a substantial part of this burden, and coins celebrated the change with an image of mules and a cart.

It was a modest-looking subject for imperial money.

Yet it expressed something important about Nerva's government. The emperor's authority could be felt not only in the Senate or the army, but in the daily obligations imposed upon people who had never seen him.

Another coin carried a message with a much more painful history.

It showed a palm tree, a symbol strongly associated with the land of Judaea, and announced the removal of an abuse connected with the Jewish tax.

To understand the importance of that announcement, it was necessary to remember the reign of Vespayzian.

After the destruction of the Temple in Jerusalem, the Flayvian government had imposed a special payment upon Jews throughout the Roman world. Money once associated with the Temple was redirected to the Roman state, and the tax endured after Vespayzian's death.

Under Domittianne, its enforcement had become particularly severe.

A person might be accused of concealing Jewish ancestry or of observing Jewish practices without publicly acknowledging them. The charge could lead to an investigation that intruded into the most private aspects of a person's life.

One later Roman writer recalled witnessing the humiliating examination of an elderly man to establish whether he was liable to pay.

It was an episode that revealed how the authority of an empire could reach into the body and identity of a single subject.

Nerva's new coin declared that the abuse had been removed.

It did not establish that the Jewish tax itself had disappeared forever. Later evidence shows that the tax continued in some form, and historians still disagree about the precise limits of Nerva's reform.

But the message of the coin was unmistakable in one respect. The new emperor wanted to distance his government from the most oppressive methods associated with the old one.

This was a different kind of imperial image from a marble statue or a triumphal arch.

The face of Nerva appeared on one side of the coin. On the other was a declaration that an abuse of power had been addressed.

Yet not all the improvements made under his government could be carried in a purse.

Across Rome, public fountains and water basins continued to provide one of the necessities upon which everyday life depended.

A woman filling a vessel at a neighbourhood fountain had little reason to concern herself with the name of the man responsible for the city's aqueducts. What mattered was that the water flowed, and that it continued to flow when her family needed it.

Behind that apparently simple act stood an immense network of channels, reservoirs, pipes and workers.

In the year ninety-seven, Nerva entrusted its supervision to Sekstus Yoolius Frontinus.

Frontinus was an experienced public servant who had governed Britain and held important offices before being appointed to oversee Rome's water supply.

He approached the responsibility with unusual care.

Rather than accept every figure recorded by his predecessors, he set about examining the system itself: its sources, the distances water travelled, the amounts supposedly supplied and the places where that supply was distributed.

The information did not always agree.

There were differences between the quantities recorded in official documents and those that could be measured in the channels. Water intended for public use had been diverted. Private landowners and dishonest workers had found ways of taking more than they were entitled to receive.

Frontinus later set down these findings in a detailed account of his administration.

He understood that a city's water supply could be weakened not only by broken stonework or neglected pipes, but by inaccurate records and the small opportunities for dishonesty that existed wherever a valuable resource had to be managed.

For Rome's inhabitants, the improvements could be experienced in the most ordinary places.

More reliable public fountains, better supervision and water restored to lawful use offered benefits that did not depend on a person's standing or wealth.

Such work lacked the spectacle of a military victory.

But it was part of what governing Rome actually meant.

And while the aqueducts continued to carry water through the city, Nerva was discovering that some of the difficulties of his reign could not be repaired through careful administration.

There were men who were not content to accept his authority simply because the Senate had recognised him.

One of them was Kalpurnius Krassus, a member of a family whose name reached back into the great political struggles of the Republic.

Krassus became involved in a plot against the emperor.

An ancient account preserves an extraordinary story of Nerva's response. At a public spectacle, the emperor is said to have seated the suspected conspirators beside him and offered them swords to examine, as though the weapons were no more than objects of interest.

If the account is substantially true, the gesture was remarkable. Nerva had deliberately placed himself in a position where the men accused of plotting against him were close enough to strike.

It was a demonstration of confidence, or perhaps of his determination not to answer every threat with an execution.

The exact circumstances are difficult to establish, and the story has the polished shape of an anecdote told to illustrate a ruler's character.

But it fitted the reputation Nerva was building.

He wished to be remembered as an emperor who did not destroy men merely because they had become dangerous to him.

There was courage in such restraint.

There was also a risk.

The emperor could refuse to punish an enemy, return a confiscated estate, or reduce the burden of a tax. He could choose what sort of ruler he wished to become.

But his choices still depended upon the willingness of others to obey.

And the men who would test that obedience most severely did not need to be admitted to the palace as guests.

They were already carrying weapons in the emperor's service.
