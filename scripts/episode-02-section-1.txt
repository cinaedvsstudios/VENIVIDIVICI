In the days following the death of Domittianne, the imperial palace had a new master.

For the men who worked within its walls, there were still messages to carry, records to maintain, and orders to be delivered. An attendant passing through the corridors might have found little changed from the previous morning. The same marble floors lay beneath his feet. The same guards occupied their posts. And the same officials continued the complicated work of governing an empire.

Yet something fundamental had changed.

For fifteen years, every important decision made within these rooms had ultimately belonged to Domittianne.

Now his name was being removed from the places where it had once commanded obedience.

On the eighteenth of September, in the year ninety-six after Christ, Domittianne had been murdered inside his own palace. The men responsible had brought an end to the Flayvian dynasty, which had ruled Rome since the civil wars of twenty-seven years earlier.

And before the day was over, another man had taken his place.

His name was Nerva.

He was sixty-five years old, an experienced senator who had spent much of his life in the company of emperors. Unlike the man he replaced, he had no dynasty to uphold, no sons waiting to inherit his position, and no great military victories to celebrate.

He had become ruler of the Roman world at an age when many men of his standing were beginning to withdraw from public life.

Outside the palace, the consequences of Domittianne's death were already becoming visible.

The Senate had turned against the memory of the dead emperor. Statues that had once represented his authority were pulled down, and his images were removed from public buildings. Some of the valuable metal portraits erected in his honour were melted down.

Men who had feared Domittianne during his lifetime could now condemn him openly.

But not everyone in Rome welcomed his destruction.

The soldiers who had guarded him, the officials who had served him, and the many people whose lives had been shaped by his government did not all share the Senate's relief.

For some, Domittianne had been a dangerous and suspicious ruler.

For others, he had been a generous employer, a capable administrator, and the emperor to whom they had sworn their loyalty.

Nerva had inherited all of them.

His full name was Markus Kokayus Nerva, and he came from a distinguished family whose connections reached back through generations of Roman public life.

His ancestors had been respected for their knowledge of law, and his family enjoyed connections with the old imperial aristocracy.

But Nerva's own career had been unusual.

He had never commanded a great army or built his reputation through conquest. His experience lay in the political world of Rome, among the senators, magistrates and imperial advisers who helped maintain the machinery of government.

And he had served under some very different rulers.

During the reign of Nero, Nerva had already become a man of influence. Following the discovery of a conspiracy against the emperor in the year sixty-five, he received the honours normally associated with a victorious military commander.

For a man without a celebrated military career, these were remarkable distinctions.

They suggested that his abilities were valued in the dangerous political world of the imperial court, where loyalty and careful judgement could be as important as service on a battlefield.

Nerva also enjoyed poetry, an interest he shared with Nero.

His talents and connections helped him remain close to power, even as the people occupying the imperial throne changed.

When Nero died and Rome descended into civil war, Nerva survived the upheaval.

In the year seventy-one, he served as consul alongside Vespayzian, the founder of the Flayvian dynasty.

It was one of the highest honours a Roman senator could receive.

Nearly twenty years later, in the year ninety, he held the consulship again.

This time, his colleague was Domittianne himself.

It was an extraordinary career. Nerva had enjoyed the confidence of emperors whose personalities, ambitions and methods of government could hardly have been more different.

By the time Domittianne was assassinated, he had spent decades navigating the changing loyalties of Roman politics.

And now, unexpectedly, he stood above the men whose favour he had once depended upon.

The Senate welcomed him.

For many senators, Nerva represented the possibility of a different relationship between the emperor and Rome's traditional governing class.

During the final years of Domittianne's reign, accusations of treason had become especially frightening. Men of wealth and influence could discover that their position offered little protection against imperial suspicion.

Some had been executed. Others had been driven into exile, while families lived with the knowledge that a careless association or an accusation could destroy everything they possessed.

The death of Domittianne had removed the man at the centre of those fears.

But it had not yet removed the fear itself.

The Senate needed more than a different face upon the throne.

It needed some assurance that the power of the emperor would be exercised differently.

And Nerva was prepared to offer one.

Before the assembled senators, he swore that he would not put a member of their order to death.

It was a striking promise from a Roman emperor.

The position he now occupied carried enormous personal authority. Previous rulers had demonstrated that the Senate's ancient privileges could offer little resistance when an emperor decided to destroy one of its members.

Nerva was publicly placing a limit upon his own conduct.

He also made a practice of consulting leading men on matters of government, rather than presenting every decision as the unquestionable command of a single ruler.

These gestures mattered.

For senators who had spent years watching their colleagues disappear, the opportunity to participate in government without the same immediate threat of execution represented a profound change.

Yet Nerva had not restored the old Roman Republic.

The emperor still controlled the central institutions of imperial government. His authority continued to reach across the provinces, and the army remained the foundation upon which Roman power ultimately rested.

The senators could speak more freely, but Nerva was still their emperor.

And governing an empire required considerably more than winning the affection of the Senate.

Within the palace, the work of administration continued.

There were provincial reports to receive, legal decisions to record, financial accounts to examine and communications to send across territories stretching from Britain to the eastern Mediterranean.

A clerk responsible for preparing official correspondence would still have found his days occupied with familiar tasks.

The government needed information. Officials needed instructions. Governors expected responses, and decisions made in Rome had to be communicated to people who might be hundreds of miles away.

The death of an emperor did not suspend any of these responsibilities.

Indeed, the sudden change of ruler made their continuation even more important.

The imperial household contained people who had served under Domittianne for many years. Some had spent much of their working lives performing duties connected with his personal administration.

Freedmen, secretaries, messengers and attendants all belonged to the complicated organisation surrounding the emperor.

Their responsibilities did not necessarily end when the man they served was replaced.

Nerva depended upon an existing system, one that had been developed and maintained by his predecessors.

He could change its leadership and alter its policies, but he could not simply discard the people who understood how it worked.

And the same was true of the soldiers.

Across the empire, Roman legions remained stationed along the frontiers, defending territory and maintaining the authority of Rome.

In the north, soldiers watched the lands beyond the Rhine. Elsewhere, their comrades occupied fortresses, guarded roads and protected the boundaries of distant provinces.

These men had served Domittianne.

For much of his reign, he had paid particular attention to the military, rewarding its service and maintaining its privileges.

The feelings of the soldiers could not be expected to change simply because senators in Rome had condemned the dead emperor.

For Nerva, this presented a difficulty that his long political career had not entirely prepared him to overcome.

He was familiar with the Senate.

He understood the relationships between influential families and the delicate business of managing political loyalties.

But he was not a soldier's emperor.

No army had followed him through a victorious campaign. His reputation had not been built among legionaries who knew him as their commander.

He had received the imperial throne through a political transition in Rome, not through the acclaim of an army.

And there were men still living who understood how dangerous that distinction could become.

One of them was Loosius Verjinnius Rufus.

When the new year began, in January of ninety-seven, Nerva took up the consulship for the third time.

His colleague was Verjinnius Rufus, a man even older than himself, whose reputation had been established almost thirty years earlier.

Rufus had spent much of his life in public service, and his name was remembered with particular respect.

But his greatest distinction was not an office he had accepted.

It was an office he had refused.

In the year sixty-eight, when Nero still occupied the imperial throne, Rufus had been commanding Roman forces in Germany.

The empire was entering a period of dangerous uncertainty.

In Gaul, a provincial governor named Julius Vindeks had risen against Nero, challenging the authority of an emperor whose behaviour and government had alienated important men across the Roman world.

The revolt brought armies into motion.

Rufus commanded powerful legions, and his soldiers became involved in the fighting around the city of Vesontio, where the forces associated with Vindeks suffered a disastrous defeat.

Vindeks died, and the rebellion in Gaul collapsed.

Yet the crisis was far from over.

Nero's position continued to deteriorate, and the question of who should rule Rome was no longer one that could be settled entirely within the imperial palace.

Rufus found himself commanding troops whose loyalty gave him extraordinary political importance.

His soldiers wanted him to become emperor.

For another commander, the offer might have been irresistible.

The support of experienced legions could place a man in a position to challenge the ruler of the Roman world.

But Rufus refused.

He would not accept the imperial throne simply because his troops wished to give it to him.

He maintained that the choice of a ruler belonged to the Senate and the Roman people.

His refusal did not remove him from danger.

In the confused struggles that followed Nero's death, armies continued to make and unmake emperors.

Galba took the throne, only to be overthrown by Otho. Otho himself was defeated by the forces of Vittellius, and the empire moved ever deeper into civil war.

Again, soldiers attempted to raise Rufus to supreme power.

And again, he refused.

His reluctance placed him in danger from men who did not understand why their commander would reject the honour they offered.

He survived, however, and eventually withdrew from the centre of political conflict.

For the following decades, Rufus lived with a reputation that few Roman senators could equal.

He had commanded armies at a time when such command might have made him emperor.

He had been offered the throne and had declined it.

And he had lived long enough to see Vespayzian establish a dynasty, Titus inherit it, and Domittianne bring that dynasty to an end.

Now, in the year ninety-seven, he stood beside Nerva as consul.

The two elderly men represented very different experiences of Roman power.

Nerva had risen through political relationships, careful service and the favour of successive emperors.

Rufus had held the loyalty of soldiers who had wanted to make him their ruler.

Yet both had lived through the civil wars that followed Nero's death.

Both understood that the authority of an emperor could depend upon forces far beyond the Senate chamber.

And both had survived to see another unexpected change upon the Roman throne.

For Nerva, choosing Rufus as his colleague was a considerable distinction.

It placed a respected figure from an earlier generation alongside the new emperor, a man whose refusal of supreme power had become central to his public reputation.

But the presence of such a man could not remove the difficulties facing Nerva.

Rome had a new emperor.

The Senate had received promises of greater security, and the political atmosphere of the capital was beginning to change.

But the soldiers remained loyal to memories of the previous reign, and the officials who had served Domittianne were still part of the government Nerva now commanded.

Within the palace, the records continued to be written.

Messages travelled between offices, accounts were examined, and instructions passed through the hands of people whose work had changed remarkably little.

The name placed upon those instructions was different.

And beyond the marble corridors of the imperial residence, the empire continued its ordinary business.

For the moment, Nerva had the support of the Senate and the authority of the imperial office.

But an emperor needed more than the approval of Rome's senators.

He needed the obedience of the men who carried weapons in his name.

And that was something Nerva had yet to secure.
